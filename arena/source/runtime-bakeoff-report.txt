Cité du Tournoi: runtime bakeoff report

## Runtime bakeoff: Hybrid Diorama vs Full 3D

Two playable prototypes of the same Ren vs Marek encounter, built over one shared combat simulation. This report describes what was built, what I actually observed, and what remains placeholder. It does not pick a winner. Updated after two further passes: richer procedural art (section 3) and a new HD-2D mode (section 8). The runtime table and the three-way comparison are from the latest build.
Open the playable prototype

## Read this first: what I could and could not observe

I tested in headless Chromium with a software rasterizer (SwiftShader, no GPU). It rendered about 3 to 4 frames per second in both prototypes, at any resolution, with or without shadows. So I could not judge how either one feels in motion, and I have no real frame-time numbers. What I did observe: stills of seven scripted moments in both versions, keyboard-driven interaction tests, and structural metrics (draw calls, triangles, texture memory, build time). Anything about animation feel and responsiveness is for you to judge in the playable link, on your own GPU.

About 1,375 lines in total, one 146 KB HTML file, Three.js r128 from cdnjs, no external art. The simulation never references sprites, meshes or cameras. I tested it separately in Node (17 assertions: determinism, timings, dodge, block, early and buffered input, recovery rules, KO).

## 3. Second procedural-design pass

The first pass exposed the weakest code-made art: crude Full 3D figures, thin sprite detail, a flat arena. This pass improved all three without changing the combat or making either renderer imitate the other. Before is on the left, after on the right.

## What changed

Hybrid sprites: tapered limbs with volume gradients and rim light, shaped torso, armor plates and bracers, bigger heads with eyes, brows and hair, cloak folds with a gold hem, runed greatsword, painted brush-texture pass.

Full 3D rig: tapered lathe limbs, shaped torso, breastplate and pauldrons, greaves, faces with eyes and brows, hair tufts, ponytail and scarf tails on spring chains, detailed sword (fuller, guard, wrapped grip), blades turned to face the camera.

Arena (both): inlaid stone and sand floor with scuffs, ashlar wall with pilasters and arches, a real Coliseum arcade, cloudy sky, seat rows, varied spectators with raised arms when they cheer, drifting dust motes.

## What it cost

Hybrid sprite generation went from about 3.5 s to about 11 s in this container.

Full 3D draw calls went from 179 to 351, then back to 192 after baking static parts per joint.

Source grew from about 1,170 to 1,375 lines.

The Full 3D sword was first edge-on to the camera and read as a black line, and Ren’s shoulder pad first looked like a beard. Both were found only by inspecting raw frames.

## 7. Technical problems discovered

No GPU available for testing. Software rendering gave about 3 to 4 fps regardless of resolution or shadows, so real performance and motion feel are unmeasured.

Sprite memory and build cost. About 31 MB of estimated texture memory per fighter at 90 px per metre, plus about 11 s of generation here (the richer painter made it about 3x slower; the browser rasterizes canvas drawing lazily, so my first timer attributed it to the wrong step). A real roster needs an offline bake, compression and streaming. Sprite resolution is also tied to camera distance, so Hybrid effectively needs a locked camera.

Sprite depth. Billboards at the same depth plane z-fight during melee overlap. I used fixed offsets (Ren in front), which is a hack and not a general rule.

Normal-map lighting from sprites gives a rounded response but is fake: no self-shadowing, and the fighters cast only a projected shadow.

Detail multiplies draw calls. The richer rig first pushed Full 3D to 351 draw calls; baking static parts per joint into one vertex-coloured mesh brought it back to 192 with an identical look. Detail in 3D needs that kind of pipeline step.

Outline hulls double draw calls and triangles, break on hard-edged boxes (needing a scale hack), and have constant world thickness, so line weight changes with camera push.

Full 3D look is lighting-sensitive. The first pass was blown out, the second pale; it took a lighting and a material change to separate fighters from the arena.

Floor UI and camera pitch. The telegraph decal vanishes at a grazing angle. Any cinematic low camera needs a fallback.

Three.js r128 (the version usable under the hosting CSP) has no CapsuleGeometry or built-in post-processing, so I used lathe capsules and no screen-space outline.

## 8. HD-2D pass (pass 3)

Following your steer toward HD-2D, I added a third mode and made it the default. Same combat, same arena, same cast; only the rendering changes. Still 100% procedural: no external art.

[image: HD-2D hero frame]

HD-2D, Ren READY: outlined pixel sprites with live pixel faces, sprite-shaped cast shadows, depth of field melting the pillars and stands, bloom, pixel floor, pixel rings.

## What HD-2D adds

Pixel sprites made by code: a dedicated pixel-art pipeline (section 9): 124 frames generated in about 1.5 s cold, 0.5 s from cache.

Pixel faces with live expressions: a 24 x 24 overlay redrawn when the expression changes. 13 expressions (calm, focus, determined, stern, smirk, shout, angry, snarl with glowing eyes, pain, grit, surprise, tired with sweat, dizzy with stars) plus blinking, all driven by combat state.

Pixel-art world: arena textures drawn, reduced and dithered, nearest-filtered.

Light: real sun and brazier lights, per-pixel sprite normals, and sprite-shaped cast shadows.

Custom post stack (Three r128 has no composer): HDR target with depth, depth-of-field with bokeh highlights and tilt, 3-level bloom, god rays, filmic grade, vignette, grain, FXAA. Each piece has a toggle in the panel.

Pixel VFX: crescent slash, star flash, pixel shock ring, square sparks, embers rising from the braziers, light-shaft cards.

Camera: slightly higher than the other modes, with a slow drift, which also helps the floor telegraph read.

## The four requested improvements

Sprite cache: atlases are stored once in IndexedDB, keyed by a hash of the art code. Sprite preparation went from about 13 s to about 1 s, and the page boots in about 4.5 s warm instead of 17 to 23 s. It falls back to generating if IndexedDB is unavailable. Verified in headless Chromium only.

Animated faces: in HD-2D and Full 3D (3D eyelids, brows, mouth shapes, sweat drops, dizzy stars). The painted-sprite mode is not animated.

Props: weapon racks with spears, swords and axes, round shields, barrels with hoops, crates, hay bales, amphorae, a training dummy, a trophy table, hanging lanterns. All static props are baked into one mesh.

Crowd: hats, hoods and caps, helmets, children, broad and slim builds, waved pennants, and a crowd wave that rolls across the stands on impacts.

## Observed in HD-2D (stills only)

Strongest atmosphere of the three: the depth-of-field and bloom make the arena feel like a lit miniature, and the fighters pop as the only sharp elements. The same blur also hides most arena and crowd detail, by design.

Gauge rings, telegraph and reaction cue stay readable, and the higher camera helps the floor decal.

Pixel faces read well on Ren at 1280 x 720. On Marek they are partly lost behind his beard and the blur, and the snarl is hard to see in stills.

The grade needed three tuning passes in the first HD-2D pass: the first was washed out and then too orange. Bloom and DOF thresholds are tuned to this scene.

The dithered floor shows some moiré at certain distances.

## Problems found in this pass

My test browser served a stale page from its own cache, so one round of measurements was out of date; I added a cache-buster and re-measured.

A string replace silently corrupted an identifier in the props code; syntax checks passed and only a real run would have caught it.

Static props first added about 250 draw calls; baking them into one mesh fixed it.

Pixel faces are a separate quad anchored to a per-frame head position stored with each sprite frame. A real pipeline would need that metadata authored for every frame.

Still unmeasured: real GPU cost of the post stack (two full-resolution HDR targets plus a bloom chain) and animation feel.

## 9. Premium pixel-art pass (pass 4)

The first HD-2D sprites were a vector drawing shrunk down, so they were pixelated but not pixel art. This pass replaces them with a pipeline that works the way pixel artists do. Still 100% code, no external art.

## The pipeline, per frame

Material IDs: the figure is drawn as flat material IDs (skin, hair, cloak, tunic, pants, boot, metal, trim, leather, blade, scarf, smear) with draw order, 3x supersampled, then reduced by majority vote per pixel. Clean, alias-free shapes.

Form light: a second pass lights each part (lit side toward the sun), so a limb is shaded across its width.

Hue-shifted ramps: every material has a 4-tone ramp: shadows drift cool and purple, highlights warm and yellow. 15 materials, 56 ramp colours, 89 distinct colours in the whole atlas.

Dither only at band edges: 4x4 ordered dither at +-5% so tones blend without noise.

Cleanup: orphan pixels removed, single holes filled.

Lines: selective outline (the neighbouring ramp’s dark tone, lighter on the lit side), 1 px internal separation lines between parts, a warm bounce rim on the shadow side, specular accents on metal.

## Around the sprites

More frames: 60 (Ren) and 64 (Marek) instead of 44 and 48, including a crescent smear with its own ramp and an energy aura on Marek’s heavy wind-up.

Secondary motion: cloak, hair and scarf sway per idle frame and stream with the lunge velocity.

Pixel grid discipline: sprite positions snap to the pixel grid, so every pixel stays the same size while fighters move.

Baked light: the shading lives in the sprite; scene lights only tint it, so the ramps survive the post stack.

World textures: median-cut limited palettes (12 to 34 colours) at a pixel density matched to the sprites, replacing the dither that caused moiré.

Pixel HUD: pixel-font chips, framed segmented HP bars, and animated pixel portraits that show the same live expression as the sprite.

Sprite lab (press L): play every clip frame by frame at 4x, inspect the material ramps and the 13 expressions.

## What looked wrong at first, and what I did about it

The first output had skin shadows turning screaming orange (the hue shift kept full saturation on light colours), so the head read as a red ball. I desaturated shadows for light materials.

Bodies were too thin for the big heads, so I added mass to torso, limbs, hands and boots.

Flat shapes sitting exactly on a tone boundary produced a checkerboard. I moved their light values to the middle of a band.

Marek’s silver hair and light skin merged into a cream blob; I changed the hair to cool silver and the skin to tan and shrank the mane.

I also introduced a bug (implicit globals shared between fighters) and fixed it before any render depended on it.

## Honest limits

The poses are still derived from a skeleton and a few keyposes, not hand-drawn key frames, so the animation will read as procedural until it is judged in motion (not done here).

Marek’s face is small and partly hidden by his beard, so his expressions are harder to read than Ren’s.

Sprite shadows are flat card projections. Pixel density of the floor matches the sprites only approximately because the floor is in perspective. Snapping is on world X only, so the slow camera drift can still shift pixels slightly.

## 10. Art-direction pass on your reference (pass 5)

You asked for a premium procedural pixel-art HD-2D combat look, with your uploaded preview as the baseline: smaller, integrated fighters; a much stronger environment; a crisp gameplay plane; and contextual camera emphasis. This pass rebuilds the HD-2D arena and camera around that direction. Everything is still generated by code.

[image: Reference vs live render]

Your reference (left) next to the live procedural render (right), same moment. The structure and scale match; the reference is still richer in material realism and skyline presence (see the gap list below).

## 1. Locked fighter scale

At the default wide shot, Ren is about 21 to 22% of the frame height (about 155 px at 720p), so one sprite pixel is about 2.2 screen pixels. That is smaller than the earlier large-character framing and slightly larger than a strict Octopath scale.

Fighters stand 4.8 m apart (was 3.1); lunges are scaled 1.75x so strikes still connect. The camera orbit is 15.4 m at an 11 degree elevation with a 32 degree field of view.

## 2. A real environment: "Arène des Champions"

Plaza: fine cobblestones with a 336 px medallion (navy and gold, fleur-de-lis, laurel and gem rings), scattered petals, ambient-occlusion strips at the wall bases and a gentle darkening toward the edges so the fighting area pops.

Architecture: parapet with pillars and finials, five-step stone tribunes on both sides with scalloped awnings, a VIP balcony with columns, canopy, balustrade and stairs, stone braziers.

Heraldry: banners with a pixel fleur-de-lis in navy and red, small ones on the parapet and tall ones on poles, all waving.

Crowd: about 170 pixel spectators from 16 authored people (hats, hoods, helmets, outfits, nobles in the balcony), with idle bobbing, a cheering frame and a wave across the stands on impacts.

City backdrop: river with moving water, a bridge with arches, a skyline with castle towers and a cathedral, trees, drifting clouds, birds, all in layered cards with distance haze.

Foreground: soft, pre-blurred silhouettes of spectators at the bottom corners.

## 3. Crisp gameplay plane, lighting hierarchy

Depth of field now has separate near and far ranges: the fighting plane stays sharp, the far backdrop is only gently soft, and the foreground blurs. Bloom, god rays and saturation were reduced from the earlier HD-2D look.

A raking sun from the left gives long fighter shadows; sprite shading is baked so it survives the grade.

## 4. Readability without bigger sprites

Silhouette contrast and selective outlines (section 9), floor rings with pips, the red floor telegraph, a glowing energy aura and orbiting red swooshes on heavy casts.

HUD in the reference idiom: title block, framed panels with pixel portraits, HP and action-gauge bars with diamond pips, a "Prochaine" line for Marek, an "Attaque puissante !" alert with a live countdown, a contextual Réaction panel (Esquiver / Bloquer, with a shrinking window bar) or "À vous de jouer" command panel, and a timeline of upcoming events with portrait nodes.

## 5. Contextual camera

Wide by default. On Marek’s heavy cast the camera pushes in (up to 1.4x), holds through the reaction window and the impact, then eases back; on Ren’s attacks it leans toward the strike; on a KO it lingers on the loser. It can be turned off in the panel.

## What I got wrong along the way

The first arena render had cobbles as wide as the characters, a sky gradient that mapped to its pale bottom band (so the backdrop looked washed out), and a skyline about three times too big.

Foreground crowd sprites were outside the narrow field of view, then turned into glitchy blocks when blurred, because giant nearest-filtered pixels do not blur smoothly. They are now soft cards.

Each textured box with a material array cost 6 draw calls, pushing the scene to 627. Batching static boxes by texture brought it to 117.

The impact flash and crescent swamped Ren at push-in zoom; I reduced them. The first energy swooshes also hid Marek, so they now sit behind him, and one referenced a variable before it was assigned.

## Honest gap list against your reference

Material realism: your reference has photographic-quality stone and lighting. Mine reads as clean, saturated, more cartoonish pixel art and is flatter in its lighting.

Skyline presence: the castle and cathedral are small and partly hidden by the tribunes, tall banners and HUD; your reference shows them large and clear.

Architecture depth: the VIP balcony and stairs are mostly out of frame; there is no large staircase mass like your reference.

VFX: the telegraph is a floor decal plus swooshes, not the large arcing energy of your reference. The sprites are still driven by a skeleton rather than hand-drawn key frames.

Not measured: real GPU cost (the test browser has no GPU) and how the camera and animation feel in motion.

Merchant District = indigo, slate and amber. Arena = warm stone, burnt gold and brown-violet shadows, with red only for the enemy and blue only for the player. Focal order: fighters, telegraphs, floor, near architecture, crowd, distant city.

## Geometry removed or simplified

Removed: the VIP balcony and stairs, tribune awnings, both pedestal braziers and their point lights, the two tall banner poles, four wall banners, parapet pillars and finials, 150 floor petals, river, bridge, trees and birds, the foreground posts.

Simplified: tribunes from 5 tiers to 3; the back wall’s arches are a texture, not geometry; the single sparse fire source is a pair of small tower-top flames and a few lantern points.

## 4. Real-device validation kit

A built-in Device test (Debug panel, or ?devtest=1) plays the arena with a bot and measures: GPU frame time (timer query where the browser offers it), frame-time percentiles and hitches, three A/B variants (post off, DOF off, FXAA off), camera push smoothness, idle sprite sub-pixel jitter and sprite pixel size, telegraph contrast read from the framebuffer (including a deuteranopia simulation), JS heap, draw calls, device and GPU strings. It reports PASS, WARN or FAIL against stated budgets and exports JSON and three key frames.

A separate checklist document gives the device matrix, the budgets for 60 and 30 fps targets, the manual protocols (thermal soak, fallback order, recognition test with 5 people, colour-blind filters, FXAA A/B, resize and DPR checks), a results table and go or no-go rules.

FXAA is now a toggle because it runs over the whole frame, sprites included, and might soften pixel edges; it is unvalidated until checked on a real device.

## 1. Combat rules as implemented (all numbers are placeholders)

Element | Behaviour |

Action points (AP) | Maximum 5, start 3. Light attack gives +1 AP when it connects (a dodged light gives nothing, so whiffing cannot farm AP). Heavy attack gives and costs nothing. |

No waiting time | There is no gauge. Any action can start at any moment; each has only a charge time. Light 0.20 s (almost instant), heavy 0.50 s (2.5x). Pressing during the last 0.2 s of another action queues it; earlier presses are ignored, not silently kept. |

Guard | Hold to cut damage by 60%, costs no AP, takes 0.10 s to come up. A heavy hit on a guarding fighter breaks the guard and stuns it for 2.4 s (you said “may be stunned”: the chance is 100% for now and is a single value to tune). Starting an action drops the guard. |

Dodge | Costs 1 AP, accepted inside the enemy’s reaction window (light 0.22 s, heavy 0.42 s) plus a 0.15 s early buffer; avoids all damage; denied with no AP; not possible while charging an action. |

Techniques (4) | Almost instant (0.16 to 0.24 s charge), each with a cooldown. Coup éclair: 2 AP, 22 dmg, 4 s. Fente perçante: 2 AP, 20 dmg, ignores guard, 6 s. Tourbillon: 3 AP, 28 dmg, stuns 1 s and interrupts, 9 s. Estocade: 4 AP, 58 dmg, 14 s. |

Enemy | No AP. It plays short sequences and then rests: see section 15 (combos of 2 to 4 hits ending in a heavy, or a single light, or a single heavy, with 5 to 10 s of rest). It cannot guard or dodge yet. Health: Ren 200, Marek 800. |

Verified by 37 automatic checks, one per sentence of your spec (costs, gains, cooldowns, denials, guard, stun, buffer, enemy chaining, KO, determinism).

## 15. Pass 10: the enemy plays sequences, then rests

The enemy must not chain attacks forever. It now plays sequences and then rests for 5 to 10 seconds. A sequence is one of three things, chosen at random with a fixed seed so the behaviour is reproducible: a combo of 2 to 4 hits whose last hit is always a heavy (60%), a single light hit (20%) or a single heavy hit (20%). The weights, the rest range, the combo length and the gap inside a combo (0.25 to 0.42 s) are all single values in the data.

[image: Enemy rhythm]

The enemy’s real attack timeline over 120 s (passive player): 12 sequences, rests between 5.2 and 9.8 s, every combo ending in a heavy hit.

## Rules and checks

Over a 900 s run (about 110 sequences) the automatic checks confirm: every sequence is 1 to 4 hits; every combo of 2 or more is light hits then exactly one heavy as the last hit; combos of 2, 3 and 4 all occur; single lights and single heavies both occur; every rest is between 5 and 10 s (mean 7.4 s); the planner is deterministic. The observed mix was 69% combos, 16% single light, 14% single heavy.

A stun (for example the Tourbillon technique) cancels the rest of a combo, and the enemy then still rests 5 to 10 s, so interrupting it is rewarded with a long opening. You did not specify this case; I kept the same 5 to 10 s rest.

The test suite is now 43 checks, all passing.

## Readability and balance

During a rest the enemy shows a relaxed idle pose and a winded face, and the panel reads “Reprend son souffle” (catching its breath); during a combo it reads “Enchaînement 2/3”, or “Attaque isolée” for a single hit. The exact rest length is not shown, so the player cannot count it down.

Side effect I caught with the balance probes: with long rests the enemy was too passive: spamming light attacks beat it, and a competent player was barely touched. I raised the enemy’s damage (light 9 to 16, heavy 24 to 48) and health (520 to 800). Now a heavy takes a quarter of Ren’s health, so the guard-break rule and the dodge cost matter.

Everything is tunable and provisional: the 60/20/20 split, the rests, and the damage and health numbers.
